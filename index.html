Backup B · Five assessment lenses

Questions worth
being ready for.

Concept checker

Why just national government?

It keeps the DV aligned with the question. An average of trust in courts, police, parties and government would be a broader construct.

Collection advisor

Why a cross-sectional survey?

It gives comparable individual measures with feasible access. Interviews add depth; a panel improves temporal ordering. The trade-off is acknowledged.

Data skeptic

Are the measures validated?

They are proposed adaptations. Pilot wording, translations and missingness; do not transfer another study’s reliability coefficient to this survey.

Rival hypothesis

Could distrust come first?

Yes. Political interest, experiences and selective participation also remain possible explanations. First VPN-use timing alone cannot resolve this.

Analysis advisor

Why Spearman rather than Pearson?

VPN categories are ordered but uneven. Spearman compares ordering and handles tied ranks. Adjusted regression is an alternative if the sample and assumptions support it; neither establishes causation.

Analysis boundary

What if the data are sparse?

Report category sizes, usable n and missingness. Omit unstable subgroup comparisons. If either variable has no variation, the correlation is undefined.

Backup C · Read-only instrument outline · No data collected here

Proposed questionnaire.

Eligibility and consent

  1. Informed consent: voluntary agreement after the study information.
  2. Currently enrolled at NU? Yes / No / Prefer not to answer.
  3. Kazakhstani citizen? Yes / No / Prefer not to answer.
  4. Aged 18–25? Yes / No / Prefer not to answer.

Continue only with consent and confirmed eligibility. No student ID or date of birth.

Core measures

  1. Trust first: How much do you trust Kazakhstan’s national government? 0–10; Don’t know; Prefer not to answer.
  2. VPN use: During the past 30 days, on how many days did you use a VPN? 0; 1–5; 6–15; 16–30.
  3. Online exposure, everyone: During the past 30 days, on how many days did you encounter online information critical of Kazakhstan’s national government? Same day categories.
  4. VPN exposure, recent users: Same question, specifying “while using a VPN.” Same categories.

Purpose and interest

  1. Recent users: For which purposes did you use a VPN? Political news; other news; entertainment/gaming; work/study; privacy/security; other. Select all.
  2. How interested are you in politics? Not at all; not very; somewhat; very.

All substantive items optional, with Don’t know / Prefer not to answer where relevant. For confirmed zero-use respondents, VPN-specific items are not applicable; missing use is not assumed to be zero.

Ask trust before potentially priming exposure questions. Pilot language versions; record no names, IDs or browsing records.

Adapted from the project’s theory, Bekmagambetov et al. (2018) and OECD (2017).

Backup D · Corrections carried forward from Part 1

Keep each finding
in its own context.

Chen & Yang: the “21.3%” claim

The published article reports a 1.58-point central-government trust difference on a 0–10 scale for the access-plus-encouragement group, described as a 21.3% relative reduction. It is not 21.3 percentage points or an effect established for Kazakhstan.

2019 article, printed p. 2322; PDF p. 29.

Golovchenko: a different outcome

The Ukrainian platform-ban study examines public posting activity. It supports the importance of access costs; it does not establish a VPN effect on political trust.

Golovchenko, Y. (2022). Fighting propaganda with censorship: A study of the Ukrainian ban on Russian social media. The Journal of Politics, 84(2), 639–654.doi:10.1086/716949 ↗

January 2022: context, not identification

Targeted blocks and complete shutdowns differ. VPNs may bypass some blocks while connectivity exists; they do not recreate a disconnected internet connection. Asking when use began cannot recover prior trust.

Reuters (2022); Access Now (2022).
